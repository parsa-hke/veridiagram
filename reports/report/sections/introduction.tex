\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A textbook diagram makes two kinds of claim. Its printed marks assert a few relations: a small square says this angle is right, two tick bars say these sides are equal. Its drawing shows many more: lines that happen to be parallel, a point on the circle, a tangent nobody flagged. The benchmarks that train and score diagram parsers, Geometry3K \cite{lu2021intergps}, PGDP5K \cite{zhang2022pgdp} and then PGPS9K \cite{zhang2023pgps}, grade on the marks, a convention inherited from the first geometry solver \cite{seo2015geos} and kept by the formal systems \cite{zhang2024formalgeo} and vision-language solvers \cite{xia2025geox} that followed. AlphaGeometry showed that a symbolic engine can supply unlimited correct supervision once a construction is stated formally \cite{trinh2024alphageometry}; nobody had asked whether such an engine could label perception.

We graded on the drawing. A coordinate verifier, a ruler laid on the page and calibrated on a hand-checked gold set, labels every candidate relation from the annotated point positions; a vision transformer \cite{dosovitskiy2021vit}, the eye, sees the diagram with the queried primitives highlighted and says which of six relations hold; the human annotation is a third witness that reports only what was marked. We ask whether the eye reads geometry from pixels when the ruler judges, and whether the ruler's labels carry the eye beyond its training distribution.
